TastePrint: A 3D Food Printing System for Layer-wise Taste Distribution via Airbrushed Liquid Seasoning

Yamato Miyatake,  Parinya Punpongsanon
Saitama University, Japan
Journal Paper PDF SCF'26 Demo (coming soon) Video BibTeX
TL;DR Instead of preparing one food ink per taste, TastePrint prints a single base ink and airbrushes liquid seasonings onto each layer as it prints. Six solenoid-driven airbrushes and a layer-wise design GUI let you place sweet, salty, sour, bitter and umami where you want them inside the food. Seasoning amount is highly predictable from spray duration (R² = 0.99), spot size is predictable from distance and duration (R² = 0.86), and tasters picked the concentrated pattern as more localized in 27 of 40 trials.

Abstract

3D food printing enables the customization of food shapes and textures, but typically produces uniform taste profiles due to the limited diversity of printable materials. We present TastePrint, a 3D food printing system that achieves layer-wise spatial taste distribution by dynamically applying liquid seasonings with a programmable airbrush during fabrication. The system integrates (1) a graphical user interface (GUI) that allows users to import 3D models, slice them into layers, and specify seasoning channels, spray positions, and intensities, and (2) a customized 3D food printer equipped with a multi-nozzle spray mechanism.

We evaluated the system through technical experiments quantifying spray resolution and deposition accuracy, a minimal sensory discrimination study on taste localization, and an exploratory formative user-feedback study involving three home cooks. The spray-resolution model achieved R² = 0.86, and the spray-amount model achieved R² = 0.99. The filter-paper calibration showed broad consistency with measurements obtained on edible mashed-potato samples. In the sensory discrimination study, participants identified the centralized seasoning pattern as more localized in 27 of 40 trials (67.5%). These findings indicate that TastePrint can provide repeatable hardware-level control over seasoning placement and quantity while offering initial evidence that spatial taste arrangement can remain perceptually meaningful after fabrication.

System

Print the shape once, then season it layer by layer

Multi-material food printers create taste variation by loading a separate ink for each flavour, so taste complexity is capped by the number of print heads, and every recipe change means preparing new inks. TastePrint separates geometry formation from taste modulation: a syringe extruder prints a single base material, and airbrushes mounted around the nozzle deposit liquid seasonings at programmed positions after each layer.

Hardware at a glance

Base printer
Creality Ender 3, custom Marlin firmware with extended G-code for airbrush activation
Extruder
30 mL syringe, 1.6 mm nozzle
Seasoning
6 × AW-102 airbrushes (0.2 mm nozzle), CKD solenoid valves, 0.10 MPa line pressure
Control
Raspberry Pi 4 + OctoPrint; 24 V DC supply; GPIO-switched valves

Materials

Base food
Mashed potato paste (water : flakes = 4 : 1), 1.6 mm layers
Five basic tastes
Sweet · sucrose Salty · NaCl Sour · citric acid Bitter · caffeine Umami · MSG
Sixth channel reserved for extensions.
Spray stabilizer
0.5 wt% xanthan gum stops nozzle dripping and gives uniform atomization (0.7 wt% clogged)

Taste Design

Click where the taste should go, layer by layer

The GUI (Python 3.11, PyQt5) imports an STL/OBJ model, slices it with the chosen layer height and nozzle diameter, and lets users assign seasoning channels, spray positions and intensities per layer. It then exports one G-code file that interleaves extrusion and spray commands.

Free selection

Manually pick spray positions and intensities for each layer. Gives full control, e.g. for chefs.

Patterned selection

Predefined spatial templates (e.g., dense packing) for quick pattern generation.

Total amount

Specify the total amount of each taste; the system allocates it across layers from the geometry and constraints.

Evaluation

How precisely can a spray place a taste?

We ran two technical experiments (spray resolution and spray amount per shot), a minimal sensory discrimination study, and an exploratory formative user-feedback study with three home cooks.

1 · Spray resolution

Dyed seasoning was sprayed onto filter paper at 20/30/40 mm nozzle-to-surface distance and 20/40/60 ms duration (0.10 MPa, 3 replicates, n = 27). Spot diameters were measured from rectified top-view images (ArUco markers, Otsu segmentation) and fitted with Size = β₀ + β₁√Distance + β₂√Duration (β₀ = −3.525, β₁ = 1.450, β₂ = 0.918). The mean SD across replicates was 0.79 mm.

2 · Spray amount per shot

Seasoning was sprayed onto a 0.001 g micro-scale at 20 mm for 10–80 ms (n = 15). Deposited mass grew linearly with duration: Amount (mg) = −0.206 + 0.082 × Duration (ms), with an average SD of 0.2 mg. So the GUI can map a desired seasoning quantity directly to a spray duration.

3 · Sensory discrimination

Ten participants each did four two-alternative forced-choice trials, comparing samples with identical geometry and nominal salt amount but different spatial distribution. The centralized sample was judged more localized in 27 of 40 trials (67.5%) (chance = 50%). Responses varied between participants. Approved by the IRB (No. R6-E-51).

4 · Exploratory user feedback

Three home cooks with no 3D-food-printing experience each completed a design-and-print session (average ≈ 15 min) and produced foods with spatially varied taste layouts. They found the layer-by-layer cross-section view helpful for reasoning about internal taste placement, saw uses in personalized meals and creative food design, and asked for more taste channels, more realistic food rendering, and a preview of the final taste distribution.

Takeaways

Geometry and taste can be designed separately

1

Duration sets the dose

Deposited mass is linear in spray duration (R² = 0.99), so a target amount of seasoning becomes a spray time.

2

Distance and duration set the footprint

Spot size grows with both. Tune them together for fine or broad taste regions, then confirm on the actual food.

3

Formulation is part of the controller

With upstream valves and open nozzles, liquid viscosity decides whether sprays drip or atomize cleanly; 0.5 wt% xanthan gum worked best here.

Scope & limitations

  • Seasoning is applied after each layer, which makes printing slower than extrusion-only workflows. Six channels limit how complex the taste combinations can be.
  • Validation used a single mashed-potato formulation. Less porous inks (e.g., meat-based) may adhere or diffuse differently, and rheological operating bounds were not quantified.
  • The spot-size model is an empirical calibration for this setup. Lateral spread over longer storage times was not studied.
  • The sensory evidence is a minimal study. Deposited mass is not the same as perceived intensity, and aroma and retronasal flavour are not addressed.
Safety. All food-contact parts were sanitized with detergent and 70% ethanol; syringes and nozzles were single-use; each seasoning had a dedicated reservoir and line. The sensory study was IRB-approved (No. R6-E-51) with informed consent.
